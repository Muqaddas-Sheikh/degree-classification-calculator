How your result is worked out

Weighted average = (Year average × its weight) added up for every year, divided by the total weight used. Example: Year 2 at 58% counting 25%, final year at 64% counting 75%:

(58 × 0.25) + (64 × 0.75) = 14.5 + 48 = 62.5% → Upper Second (2:1)

The question every final-year student asks

“My Year 2 average is 58% and it counts for 25% of my degree. What do I need in final year for a 2:1?”

Answer: 60.7%. The maths: a 2:1 needs 60% overall. Year 2 already banks 58 × 0.25 = 14.5 points, so your final year must supply 60 − 14.5 = 45.5 points across its 75% weight: 45.5 ÷ 0.75 = 60.7%.

How UK degree classifications actually work

Your final class isn’t just “your average” — it’s a weighted average, and the weighting is where most students get caught out.

Year 1 usually doesn’t count

At most UK universities, first-year marks only need to pass (40%) to progress. Your classification is normally built from Year 2 and your final year only — which is why a weak Year 1 rarely ruins a degree, and a strong final year can rescue a lot.

The final year counts most

Common weightings are 25/75, 33/67 and 40/60 (Year 2 / final year). A single final-year module can move your overall average far more than a Year 2 module did — especially large-credit projects and dissertations.

Credits weight your modules

Within a year, modules are weighted by credits. A 40-credit module at 65% pulls your average up twice as hard as a 20-credit module at the same mark. Always check the credit value before panicking — or celebrating — over one result.

Borderline students may be uplifted

Many universities review students within about 2% of a boundary (for example 58–59.9% for a 2:1). If most of your final-year credits sit in the higher class, your board may award the higher classification. Rules vary by university — this calculator shows the straight weighted average.

Common questions

What average do I need for a 2:1?

60% overall — but “overall” means your weighted average across the years that count, not a simple mean of every mark you’ve ever received. If your Year 2 average is below 60%, you can still finish with a 2:1 if your final year (which usually counts for more) pulls the weighted average up to 60. Use the “What do I need?” tab to get your exact number.

My Year 2 average is 58%. Can I still get a First?

Possibly — it depends on the weighting. With Year 2 at 25% and the final year at 75%, you’d need (70 − 58 × 0.25) ÷ 0.75 = 74% in your final year. That’s demanding but done every year. With a 40/60 weighting you’d need 78% — a much taller order. Run your own numbers in the reverse calculator.

Do all universities use the same weightings?

No. 25/75, 33/67 and 40/60 are the most common patterns for Year 2 / final year, some universities use the best 100 credits of your final year, some use a “exit velocity” or majority-of-credits rule at borderlines, and a few count Year 1 slightly. Always confirm your own university’s regulations in your course handbook — then set those exact weightings here.

What happens if I’m just below a boundary — like 59.4%?

Many universities have a borderline policy: if your weighted average falls within a set margin (often 2%) below a boundary and a majority of your final-year credits are at the higher class, the exam board can award the higher classification. It’s discretionary and university-specific, so a 59.4% is not a guaranteed 2:1 — but it’s often worth checking your regulations before assuming the lower class.

How is a masters classification different?

Masters degrees use three outcomes instead of four: Distinction (70%+), Merit (60–69%) and Pass (50–59%). Below 50% is a fail — there’s no equivalent of a Third. The weighted-average maths is identical; select “Masters” in the calculators to use these boundaries.

Is a 2:2 a bad degree?

No. A 2:2 is a full honours degree and a large share of UK graduates receive one. Some large graduate schemes filter at 2:1, but many employers, most SMEs and most postgraduate courses (with experience) consider 2:2 graduates. Your class is one line on your CV — placements, projects and experience carry real weight alongside it.
